# Inside the neuron: understanding $y = Wx + b$

*Created: 2026-11-23 · Last updated: 2026-11-23*

> **The idea in one sentence.** A neural-network layer takes several input numbers, mixes them using *weights* that **you do not write by hand: they are learned**, and produces new numbers. Everything else in deep learning is built by stacking and tuning this operation.

---

## 0. The story behind the formula

Imagine you must decide whether a robot should brake. You receive three signals: the **distance** to an obstacle, the robot's **speed**, and the **slope** of the terrain. They do not all weigh the same: a short distance is very dangerous, a high speed makes things worse, and the slope matters little.

A natural way to decide is a **weighted vote**: multiply each signal by a number that measures its importance, add everything up, and include a fixed adjustment depending on how "cautious" you want to be. That, and nothing more, is a neuron:

$$
y = w_1 x_1 + w_2 x_2 + w_3 x_3 + b
$$

The underlying question of machine learning is: *how do I find good values for the $w_i$ and for $b$?* That is the part that is learned from data. Today we focus on understanding **what** the formula does and **why** it has this shape.

---

## 1. The pieces and their dimensions

For a layer with $n$ inputs and $m$ output neurons:

| Symbol | What it is | Belongs to |
|---|---|---|
| $x$ | input vector | $\mathbb{R}^{n}$ |
| $W$ | weight matrix | $\mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$ |
| $b$ | bias vector | $\mathbb{R}^{m}$ |
| $y$ | output vector | $\mathbb{R}^{m}$ |

The full formula is

$$
y = Wx + b .
$$

**Golden rule so you don't get lost:** the product $Wx$ only exists if the columns of $W$ match the size of $x$. That is, $(m \times n)\cdot(n \times 1) = (m \times 1)$. When a network fails with an "incompatible shapes" error, it is almost always this rule.

---

## 2. The simplest case: a single neuron ($m = 1$)

With a single output, $W$ is one row, which we write as $w^\top$:

$$
y = w^\top x + b = \sum_{i=1}^{n} w_i x_i + b .
$$

**Numerical example.** Let

$$
x = \begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \\ 3 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad
w = \begin{pmatrix} 0.5 \\ -1 \\ 2 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad
b = 1 .
$$

Then

$$
y = (0.5)(2) + (-1)(1) + (2)(3) + 1 = 1 - 1 + 6 + 1 = 7 .
$$

**How to read each number:**

- **The sign of $w_i$** tells whether that input *pushes the output up* ($w_i > 0$) or *down* ($w_i < 0$).
- **The size of $|w_i|$** tells *how much that input matters*.
- **The bias $b$** is the value of the output when all inputs are zero. It works as a threshold: it shifts the decision point without depending on the data.

### Why do we need $b$?

Without $b$, if the input is $x = 0$ the output is always $0$. The neuron would be forced to pass through the origin. The bias gives it the freedom to **shift**. We will see this clearly in the geometry.

---

## 3. The geometry: a neuron cuts space in two

Recall that the dot product is related to the angle between vectors:

$$
w^\top x = \|w\|\,\|x\|\cos\theta .
$$

Therefore, $w^\top x$ measures **how much $x$ "points" in the direction of $w$**.

Now look at the set of points where the neuron equals zero:

$$
w^\top x + b = 0 .
$$

In $\mathbb{R}^2$ it is a **line**; in $\mathbb{R}^3$, a **plane**; in general, a **hyperplane**. And it has a very clear reading:

- The vector $w$ is **perpendicular** to that hyperplane: it defines its orientation.
- The bias $b$ determines **how far from the origin** it lies.
- The quantity

$$
d(x) = \frac{w^\top x + b}{\|w\|}
$$

is the **signed distance** from $x$ to the hyperplane: positive on one side, negative on the other.

**Geometric conclusion:** each neuron splits the input space into two halves and tells each point which side it is on and how far away it is. That is why $b$ matters: without it, the hyperplane would be tied to the origin.

---

## 4. Many neurons: the matrix $W$

With $m$ neurons, you stack $m$ rows:

$$
W = \begin{pmatrix}
- & w_1^\top & - \\
- & w_2^\top & - \\
 & \vdots & \\
- & w_m^\top & -
\end{pmatrix}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
y_k = w_k^\top x + b_k , \; k = 1,\dots,m .
$$

**Numerical example.** With $x$ as before and

$$
W = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & -1 \\ 2 & 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad
b = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix},
$$

$$
Wx = \begin{pmatrix} (1)(2) + (0)(1) + (-1)(3) \\ (2)(2) + (1)(1) + (0)(3) \end{pmatrix}
= \begin{pmatrix} -1 \\ 5 \end{pmatrix},
\qquad
y = Wx + b = \begin{pmatrix} -1 \\ 6 \end{pmatrix}.
$$

### Two ways to read $Wx$

**By rows (the "neural" reading).** Each row of $W$ is a neuron; each component of $y$ is the response of one neuron. This is the reading we just used.

**By columns (the "algebraic" reading).** $Wx$ is a *linear combination of the columns of $W$*, with coefficients given by $x$:

$$
Wx = x_1\, W_{:,1} + x_2\, W_{:,2} + \dots + x_n\, W_{:,n} .
$$

Check with the example: the columns are $(1,2)$, $(0,1)$ and $(-1,0)$, so

$$
2\begin{pmatrix}1\\2\end{pmatrix} + 1\begin{pmatrix}0\\1\end{pmatrix} + 3\begin{pmatrix}-1\\0\end{pmatrix}
= \begin{pmatrix}2\\4\end{pmatrix} + \begin{pmatrix}0\\1\end{pmatrix} + \begin{pmatrix}-3\\0\end{pmatrix}
= \begin{pmatrix}-1\\5\end{pmatrix} .
$$

Both readings are true at the same time, and switching between them is a very useful skill.

---

## 5. Linear vs. affine: the exact role of $b$

The function $x \mapsto Wx$ is a **linear map**: it satisfies $T(x+z) = T(x) + T(z)$ and $T(cx) = cT(x)$, and in particular it sends the origin to the origin.

The function $x \mapsto Wx + b$ is **not linear** in the strict sense: it is **affine** (a linear map followed by a translation). This is a precision detail worth keeping clear: in the AI literature it is called a "linear layer" out of habit, but mathematically it is affine.

### A trick that connects to robotics

You can absorb the bias into the matrix by appending a $1$ to the input:

$$
\tilde{x} = \begin{pmatrix} x \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad
\tilde{W} = \begin{pmatrix} W & b \end{pmatrix}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
y = \tilde{W}\,\tilde{x} .
$$

This is called **homogeneous coordinates**, and it is exactly the device used in robotics to combine a rotation and a translation in a single matrix. The same mathematical idea appears in both worlds.

---

## 6. Processing many data points at once (batches)

In practice you do not process one vector but a **batch** of $N$ examples. They are stacked as the rows of a matrix $X \in \mathbb{R}^{N\times n}$ and we compute

$$
Y = X W^\top + \mathbf{1}\, b^\top, \qquad Y \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times m},
$$

where $\mathbf{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}$ is a vector of ones (the same bias is added to every row; in NumPy this is called *broadcasting*). The dimensions work out like this: $(N\times n)(n\times m) = N\times m$.

> **Convention.** Many texts write $y = Wx + b$ with $x$ as a column; libraries such as PyTorch store $W$ with shape (outputs, inputs) and compute $xW^\top + b$ for rows. It is the same thing written in two ways; what matters is to always check the shapes.

---

## 7. Why a single layer is not enough (and why it matters)

What happens if you stack two affine layers?

$$
y = W_2\left(W_1 x + b_1\right) + b_2
= \underbrace{(W_2 W_1)}_{W'}\,x + \underbrace{(W_2 b_1 + b_2)}_{b'} .
$$

The result is **another affine layer**. Stacking affine layers, with nothing else, **adds no representational power**: two layers, ten or a hundred are equivalent to a single one. That is why networks insert a **nonlinearity** between layers (for example, $\text{ReLU}(z) = \max(0, z)$ applied component by component). That piece is the next topic, and it is what really allows learning complicated functions.

---

## 8. What is learned, and how many parameters are there?

The layer's **parameters** are $W$ and $b$: $mn + m$ numbers in total. For a classic example, classifying images of $28\times 28$ pixels ($n = 784$) into $10$ classes ($m = 10$):

$$
784 \cdot 10 + 10 = 7850 \text{ parameters.}
$$

Training means searching for the $7850$ values that make the outputs match the correct answers as well as possible. How they are searched for (the gradient and descent) comes later.

---

## 9. What this is useful for later

- **Linear regression:** it is a single neuron. If you already know least squares, you already know the simplest case of training this layer.
- **Deeper networks:** they are this operation repeated, with nonlinearities in between.
- **Vision (CNNs):** a convolution is a $Wx + b$ layer with a highly structured $W$ (the same weights reused).
- **Transformers:** the query, key and value projections are $Wx$ layers.
- **Robotics:** perception and control use these layers, and homogeneous coordinates connect directly to kinematics.
- **Finance:** a linear factor model is, once more, $Wx + b$.

---

## 10. Mental checklist (what to always verify)

1. How many inputs $n$ and how many outputs $m$ do I have?
2. Does $W$ have shape $m \times n$ and does $b$ have size $m$?
3. Do I understand each row of $W$ as a neuron with its hyperplane?
4. Am I treating the layer as linear when it is actually affine?

## Summary

$$
\boxed{\;y = Wx + b\;}
$$

- Each row of $W$ is a **neuron**: a weighted vote of the inputs.
- Geometrically, each neuron defines a **hyperplane** that splits space in two and measures signed distance.
- $b$ **shifts** that hyperplane; without it, everything would be anchored to the origin.
- The operation is **affine**, not strictly linear, and stacking it as is equals a single layer: that is why a **nonlinearity** is needed.
- $W$ and $b$ are what the network **learns**.
